Production deployed. Still a pilot-stage product.

A lit, governed home for everything a student club runs. One event record, from the first draft to the record a student keeps.

WayClub is the approval and event-operations layer that lets student clubs plan, get approved and run events in one place, while universities govern the process with confidence. This is its public engineering showcase: the build repository is private, and every number here was read from that code, that database catalogue or a test run. The live product currently serves from Vercel and Fly.io.

The problem

Approvals live in an inbox, and the event lives in six places

Student clubs coordinate events, approvals, members and communication across email, WhatsApp, spreadsheets, forms, shared drives and disconnected portals. Approvals are slow, documents go missing, ownership is unclear, and nobody can say who agreed to what. Off-campus and higher-risk events are worst, because they are exactly the ones that need a paper trail.

The research behind WayClub is grounded in a Malaysian private university's student council town hall of November 2024, which attributed club event-approval delays to safety concerns and administrative processes, with off-campus events requiring additional scrutiny. The same university's clubs and societies manual requires a proposal at least one month before a major event, routed from committee to advisor to student services. None of the tools in that chain were built for it.

The design decision everything else serves

The event is one durable record

The proposal, the multi-stage approval, the poster, registration, the entry fee, attendance, the photographs taken on the day, the post-event report and the student's verified involvement record all attach to the same object, through fifteen server-owned states from draft to archived. Information entered once carries forward. Nothing is re-keyed between an email thread and a spreadsheet, and nothing is lost between them.

What a real person can finish today

Each line below is a journey somebody can complete end to end in the running application, not a table that exists in the schema.

A student

  • Browses clubs with each club's trust state in plain language, joins an open one or applies to an approval-based one and answers that club's own questions
  • Discovers events under day headers with posters, entry-fee chips and going counts
  • Saves an event without committing to attend, privately
  • Registers, with capacity and a waitlist
  • Checks in with an eight-character attendance code
  • Uploads a photograph to the event's gallery once it is live
  • Holds a verified involvement record and downloads it as a document

A committee member

  • Writes a multi-step proposal that autosaves, with a poster and required alt text
  • Submits it into an approval route chosen by the answers given
  • Revises after changes are requested, and the route is re-evaluated against what the event now is
  • Walks the event from approved to scheduled, open, live, completed, reported and archived
  • Runs check-in, then writes a report pre-filled from real attendance rows
  • Issues participation records and certificates to verified attendees
  • Sets club jobs with due dates and assignees, and shapes the club's own look

A reviewer

  • Works a queue sorted by service-level due date, filtered by status, club, risk and overdue
  • Reads full event context on one screen
  • Sees a field-level diff of what changed since the last submission, in sentences
  • Is warned in words when the risk level moved, because that changes who must approve
  • Approves, requests changes with a mandatory comment, or rejects
  • Delegates the queue to a named substitute for a bounded period before going away
  • Sees a substitute's decisions marked as made on the delegator's behalf

A president, an advisor, a director

  • Records an election or an appointment as an outcome, with evidence
  • Forms the incoming term and initiates a handover with role-specific checklists
  • Resolves ownership risks and completes a two-party attestation that no secret is ever part of
  • Executes an effective-date authority transfer, in the browser
  • Opens oversight and reads what is blocked, who is waiting on whom, and what is overdue
  • Is told plainly when the answer is that they oversee nothing

The refusal that shapes the product

WayClub records an entry fee and says who collects it. It never processes a payment. The fee tile on an event page reads, verbatim: "Collected by {club}, not by WayClub." There is no ranking, no leaderboard, no social feed, no internal chat, no accounting ledger, no biometric attendance and no predictive analytics on students. The rule is that if mishandling the data could seriously harm a student, WayClub does not store it.

The identity

A violet-black world with something lit in it

The first design pass measured a reference product honestly and produced something competent and anonymous. The owner rejected it, redesigned the event card himself, and supplied a runnable mockup drawn over a real WayClub screenshot. That mockup is now the authority: where it and any written description disagree, the mockup wins.

The override is the owner's, and it is recorded as an architecture decision rather than left as a source comment somebody would later read as a regression.

Per-club colour

One hue in, a whole room out

A club publishes an accent in its customisation studio and an OKLCH engine derives the rest: canvas, inks, hairlines and action colour, every one contrast-gated. Inside a club's own space that palette takes over. On a mixed surface, home, discover, the calendar, the page stays WayClub's and the variety comes from the artwork, because a feed repainted per row is a rainbow.

A club sets two hues and nothing else. The strengths are platform-owned, so no club can hand its page a brighter glow than the page next door, and the anatomy is never repainted.

Calm Operations

The dean does not get a sunset

Reviews, oversight and administration are excluded from the atmosphere structurally rather than by convention: a club room mounted anywhere inside one of those surfaces cannot tint it, because the exclusion restates every themeable property at higher specificity and the ambient layer vetoes itself when a Calm Operations marker is anywhere in the document.

A test called "Calm Operations never tints, structurally" pins it, so the rule cannot quietly decay into a convention.

The mark

A waypoint, not a letter

The previous mark was a bold W whose right arm finished higher than its left. It was competent and it was anonymous: drawing the letter the name starts with is what a product does when it has not found an idea. The mark is now two brackets framing a single lit point, a place marked, a point on a way. The brackets are always WayClub's; the point can carry a club's own hue. It is the same glyph in the navigation, the browser tab and the app icon, so there are not three drawings to drift apart.

Tenancy and integrity

One mistake must never equal one breach

Cross-tenant information disclosure is the critical risk for a platform holding student data for many organisations, so the boundary is enforced where a forgotten application check cannot reach it: in the database. The tenant root is the account, not the institution, because a club must be able to adopt WayClub without its university being a customer.

  • Deny by default, in two layers Row-level security is enabled on every tenant-scoped table in the same migration that creates it. 174 policies across 63 tables, every one keyed on account_id, asserted mechanically against pg_policy rather than against a hand-kept list. The other 26 tables have row-level security on, no policies and no grants, so an unbuilt surface fails at the grant layer first and the policy layer second.
  • Identity travels with the transaction Each request runs as a Postgres role that owns nothing and holds no bypass, inside one transaction that first sets the caller's verified claims as a transaction-local setting. Policies read them through a shim shaped exactly like hosted Supabase, so pooled connections cannot leak identity between requests and a later move to hosted Postgres is a connection-string swap.
  • A club cannot impersonate an institution, structurally Every tenant-scoped row carries a composite foreign key binding its institution_id to its own account. That is a constraint, not a policy, so it binds the table owner and the bypass role exactly as it binds the request role. A standalone account owns no institution row, so it cannot write an institution reference anywhere, cannot hold an official reviewer role, and cannot record an approval carrying a university's authority. Verified: even the owning role gets SQLSTATE 23503.
  • 404, indistinguishable from not-found A request for another tenant's row returns 404, byte-identical to a request for a row that never existed, so tenants cannot be enumerated. The test that proves it sits directly beside the test proving that a same-tenant outsider gets 403 with a permission-denied code, which is what makes the distinction real rather than incidental.
  • An append-only audit log, chained per account Every decision, transition and permission change writes a row committing to the hash of the previous row for the same account. No role holds update or delete, and a trigger blocks both even for the table owner. The chain forks under concurrency if position is allocated outside the lock, which it once was; position is now assigned inside the lock at commit time, with a unique constraint as the structural backstop, and a test that genuinely overlaps two transactions rather than relying on a serial suite.
  • Saving an event is private by construction One select policy, ending in user_id = auth.uid(). No audit row is written, because the log is administrator-readable and would hand back exactly what the policy refuses. There is no saver count anywhere, because "twelve people saved this" is a demand signal, and demand signals are the pressure the product boundaries exist to refuse.

What an adversarial review found

Design claims are worth little until somebody tries to break them, so the build was put through a three-lens adversarial review whose brief was to refute these claims rather than confirm them. It proved eighteen defects with reproducible output. The tenancy layer held: cross-tenant sweeps leaked nothing in either direction, sessions carrying no claims read zero rows everywhere, tokens with no algorithm or the wrong secret were rejected, and double approvals were blocked by a unique constraint rather than by application logic.

The workflow layer did not. An organiser could take an event approved on the low-risk route, edit it during a revision cycle into an off-campus, high-risk, over-threshold event, resubmit, and keep the original two-stage route: safety, facilities and finance never saw it. The same content submitted fresh produced five approval stages; smuggled through a revision it produced two. All eighteen are now closed, each re-verified by replaying the reviewer's own exploit rather than by trusting a fix report. Two more were found during that verification, including a one-second blind spot in session revocation that an existing test had been hiding behind a sleep.

Accessibility

A gate, not a checklist

WCAG 2.2 AA is a release requirement on the same footing as the isolation tests. The colour part of it is enforced by a build that refuses to produce output when a value fails, so a designer cannot sign off around it and a reviewer cannot forget.

Per build

562 contrast checks

156 pair checks, 38 composited wash checks and 368 real-stack checks over the lit page, the card, and both glass fills over white artwork and over black, because a photograph can be anything. Worst text pair 4.61:1 against a 4.5:1 minimum.

Per club

660 more

Eighteen adversarial accents are derived into full themes (380 checks) and then used to light a page (280 checks), every fixed ink, link, danger text, border and focus ring measured on the lit canvas rather than on the flat one.

Refused

4 of 11 accents

Accent validation is real rather than advisory: of eleven probe colours the build accepts seven and refuses four. A club that submits an unusable accent is told so, with a reading, rather than quietly given a page nobody can read.

  • No status by colour aloneEvery state pairs colour with a word and usually an icon. The lit dot on a card renders only beside the words "Happening now". A revision diff strikes the old value through and also says "changed from … to", so meaning never rides on styling.
  • Keyboard-completeEvery flow is operable by keyboard alone. Two Playwright specs walk the reviewer and continuity screens for the skip link, focus visibility, keyboard reach, both themes and status-in-words.
  • Reduced motion is honoured properlyCollapsing a transition duration still lets a transform land, so the card's hover lift is removed outright rather than merely shortened, and the ambient cross-fade is not registered at all.
  • The accessible path is the primary pathCheck-in is manual lookup plus an eight-character code, which a QR design would have needed as a fallback anyway. Building it first means the accessible route got the engineering attention rather than the leftovers.
  • Not claimedThere is no axe dependency anywhere in the repository, so there is no automated accessibility scan in the pipeline, and no screen-reader pass has been recorded. Conformance is only ever claimed alongside recorded evidence, and none exists for those two.

Honest status

What is built, what is half-built, and what is only a shape

This section follows one rule: claim only what exists at each tier, and never borrow wording from the tier above. A showcase that admits its gaps is worth more than one that hides them, and the build repository's own documents already work this way.

Built

  • The core loop end to end on the web: discover, join, propose, route, revise, register, check in, close out, issue records, hand over
  • A public landing page, open sign-up with any email, founder-first club creation, multi-workspace switching, and club-role invites by email
  • Account tenancy with deny-by-default row-level security, and the structural bar on institutional impersonation
  • The versioned workflow engine, with routing re-evaluated on every resubmission
  • Append-only per-account hash-chained audit written by database triggers
  • Leadership continuity, at the database, API and web layers
  • Standalone club mode: a club with no university can get an event approved through its own internal route
  • Per-club theming, poster-derived light, and the design token gate
  • Committee tasks, announcements, files, the event gallery, oversight reads, reviewer delegation
  • Production deployment: Vercel for the web app, Fly.io for the API, hosted Supabase for the database, plus the storage, SMTP, health and single-instance controls below

Partly built

  • Saving an event works on every card and the state rides every listing, but no screen lists what a person saved
  • Reviewer queue filters and sort exist; they do not survive navigating into a stage and back, which the research names as the most-cited reviewer frustration
  • The institution claim and club migration can be negotiated but not executed: the transactional data move is unwritten
  • Notifications reach an in-app inbox and real email; there are no per-person channel preferences, no digests, no push
  • Accessibility: the contrast gate and two keyboard walks are the whole of the automated coverage

Not built

  • The mobile app. Its directory is a README, deliberately, and its scope is the full product on a phone rather than a check-in companion
  • QR check-in and the offline attendee queue, which belong to that app. The token design is pinned so the implementation cannot drift, and the substrate table is deliberately unreachable until then
  • Twenty-six tables are shape, not delivery: budgets and reimbursements, meetings and minutes, venue and asset booking, club formation and registration, general requests, notification preferences, event templates, calendar items, imports, retention, verified student identity
  • Interface translation. Money and time are already international; the words are not
  • Mobile, QR check-in and the offline attendee queue are still unbuilt
  • No production backup has ever been restored, and nothing has been load tested

Two things that would be easy to leave out

Deployed does not mean production-proven. The hosted stack is live, but one backup restore and one end-to-end upload and download through the hosted bucket still need to be proved and recorded. The current sender is also still a test sender (`WayClub <onboarding@resend.dev>`) until a verified domain is configured.

No pilot has run. There is no usage, no adoption, no measured reduction in approval time, no performance under load and no institutional endorsement. The demo tenant is named "Sunway Pilot (unofficial demo)" and implies no affiliation with, or endorsement by, any institution.

The stack

A pnpm monorepo. A NestJS 11 modular monolith serving a pinned REST contract, a Next.js App Router web app with Tailwind and TanStack Query, PostgreSQL 17 reached through explicit SQL with no ORM that could hide row-level security, and a cross-platform design token package that compiles to CSS, TypeScript and Dart. The workflow engine is a versioned in-application state machine: no Temporal, no Camunda, no external authorisation service. Human approval workflows measured in days are rows and transactions, not a distributed-systems problem.
